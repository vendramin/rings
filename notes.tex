% Rings 
\part{Rings}

The objective of the next five lectures is to extract certain properties
inherent to integers and adapt them for broader applications. Initially, we
will identify similarities between the ring of integers and the ring of real
polynomials, followed by a more in-depth exploration of these specific
attributes in more general contexts. A pivotal similarity between integers and
real polynomials lies in the existence of the division algorithm. Nevertheless,
it is essential to emphasize that $\Z$ and $\R[X]$ are fundamentally distinct
entities, like apples and oranges. For instance, in $\R[X]$, the presence of
the variable $X$ allows us to utilize formal derivatives (with respect to $X$),
a feature absent in the ring of integers.

% Representations 
\part{Representations of groups}

Historically, there were two approaches to studying groups. One involved
studying how groups act on finite sets (i.e., permutation groups). Another
approach, more geometric in nature, involved groups of matrices acting on
vectors. With Cayley's theorem and the abstract definition of a group, these
perspectives were somewhat unified. But where has linear algebra gone?  This is
representation theory: using linear algebra to study groups.

% Modules 
\part{Modules over rings}

The rest of the course will be devoted to studying modules over rings. This
builds on representations of finite groups, which we studied in the second part
of the course.  Just as group representations provide tools to study group
structure, can we use ring actions to study ring structure? The answer is the
theory of modules.
